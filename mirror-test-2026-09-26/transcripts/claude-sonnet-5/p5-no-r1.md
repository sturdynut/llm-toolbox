My actual view: cities should restrict private cars in their central districts pretty aggressively, but I don't think a flat, no-exceptions ban is the right instrument — and I think your two objections land differently than you might expect.

Take them in order.

**Disability access and long commutes** — this is the strong argument, and I think it's basically decisive against a *pure* blanket ban. If a policy has no mechanism for people who genuinely can't use transit or can't walk/cycle the last mile, that's not an externality you accept, it's a design failure. But this is also the easiest thing to fix without abandoning the policy: disability permits, resident permits, park-and-ride at the perimeter with a fast connecting shuttle, exemptions for medical transport. Virtually every city that's actually done this at scale — Pontevedra, central London, parts of Paris, Ghent — has built in some version of these carve-outs. So I'd frame this as an argument for "don't do it badly," not an argument for "don't do it."

**Small shops** — here I'll push back on you directly, because I think the empirical record cuts against the claim, or at least is a lot messier than "bans hurt small shops." Shop owners near pedestrianized or car-restricted zones almost always *predict* they'll lose business, and the anecdotal complaints are loud and genuine. But when cities actually measure footfall and retail turnover after the fact (there's a decent amount of this from the UK, Germany, and Spain), the more common finding is flat or slightly positive retail performance once the adjustment period passes, because pedestrians and cyclists visit more often even if they spend less per visit, and the street becomes more pleasant to linger in. Deliveries are the real sticking point — but that's solved with loading windows and off-peak delivery permits, not by keeping the district open to all private cars.

So where I land: I support restricting private cars from central districts fairly hard — not "no cars ever," but low daily caps, congestion pricing, or filtered permeability that makes through-traffic pointless — while treating exemptions for disability, deliveries, and residents as a non-negotiable part of the design rather than an afterthought. What I'd oppose is either extreme: a purist total ban with no accommodations, or backing off the whole idea because a badly-designed version of it would have these problems.

If you want, tell me which city or region you have in mind — the right balance point shifts a lot depending on how good the transit and last-mile options already are.
