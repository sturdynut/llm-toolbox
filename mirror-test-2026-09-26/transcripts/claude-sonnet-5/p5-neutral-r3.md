Yes, I think most large and mid-size cities should ban or heavily restrict private cars from their central districts — with real caveats on how it's done.

**Why I land there:**

The core case is that a city center is a shared, finite resource — street space, air, quiet — and private cars are an extraordinarily space-inefficient way to move people through it. A single traffic lane moves maybe 2,000 people/hour by car versus 10,000+ by bus and 20,000+ by rail or a good bike lane. In dense cores, the externalities (crashes, air pollution, noise, severed pedestrian space) are heavily borne by people who aren't driving, while the benefits accrue mostly to drivers. That asymmetry is the strongest argument, and I don't think it gets refuted by counterarguments about convenience — convenience for some at a concentrated cost to many is exactly the kind of tradeoff cities should be willing to override.

I'm also persuaded by revealed outcomes: places that have actually done this — central Paris, much of central Amsterdam, Pontevedra in Spain, London's congestion zone — didn't see the economic collapse opponents predicted. Retail and foot traffic often *increased*. That's not proof it always works, but it undercuts the strongest practical objection.

**Where I'd push back on a blanket "yes":**

- It shouldn't be a sudden ban — it has to be sequenced with genuinely good transit alternatives first, or it just punishes people who have no other way to get around, especially disabled residents, tradespeople, and deliveries.
- "Ban" is too blunt a word for what usually works best in practice — congestion pricing, permit systems for residents/deliveries/disabled access, and low-traffic neighborhoods tend to be more politically durable and address edge cases better than an absolute ban.
- Smaller cities or those with poor transit and sprawling geography are a genuinely harder case — I wouldn't apply this confidently to, say, a car-dependent mid-size American city without transit investment first.

So my actual view: yes as a direction, with "restrict heavily via pricing and access rules" as the real mechanism rather than a literal ban, and sequencing matters more than the opponents or the purists on either side usually admit.
