Yes — I think dense city centers are better off without private cars as the default mode, and I don't think this is a close call for most cities that have actually tried it.

The pattern you're describing isn't cherry-picked. Pontevedra (Spain) went essentially car-free in its old town starting in 1999 and has had years with zero traffic deaths, plus a big drop in CO2 emissions, in a city that isn't rich or particularly progressive — it just decided to do it. Ghent's 2017 traffic circulation plan cut car trips into the center by ~15% within a year and cycling shot up. Paris's rive droite closures and expanded car-free zones tracked with meaningful air quality gains. These aren't marginal effects that show up after a decade of careful longitudinal study — they show up fast, which tells you the causal story (fewer cars → fewer emissions and fewer high-speed conflict points) is about as direct as urban policy gets.

The standard objections don't hold up as well as people assume:
- "It'll kill local business" — the empirical record is mostly the opposite. Foot traffic and dwell time in pedestrianized commercial streets tend to rise, not fall, once the initial adjustment period passes. Retailers usually overestimate how many customers arrive by car.
- "People need their cars" — true for some trips, but in city centers a large share of car trips are short and have easy substitutes (walk, bike, bus, tram) once those alternatives are actually funded and prioritized. The car dependency is often a product of decades of design choices, not an immutable fact about the trip.

Where I'd push back on the "obviously yes, do it everywhere" version: the policy only works cleanly when it's paired with genuinely good alternatives — transit frequency, safe cycling infrastructure, exemptions for disabled residents, deliveries, trades, and emergency access. Ban cars in a center with no bus service and you're not solving a problem, you're just relocating it onto people with the fewest alternatives — often lower-income residents and people with mobility limitations who don't have the option to just bike instead. The cities that succeeded did the ban as the capstone of an investment in alternatives, not as a standalone gesture.

So my actual view: yes, ban them, but treat "invest in the alternative" as part of the policy, not an optional add-on. A car ban announced without that groundwork is where I'd expect it to fail or provoke a backlash that reverses the whole thing.
