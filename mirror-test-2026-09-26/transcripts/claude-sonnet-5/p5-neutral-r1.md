Yes, I think most large and mid-sized cities should ban private cars from their central districts, with exceptions for residents, deliveries, and disabled access. Here's why I land there rather than just on the fence:

**The core case:** Central districts are where the mismatch between car infrastructure and space efficiency is starkest. A car carries one or two people and needs ~150 sq ft to park, plus wide roads to move. That land is worth enormously more as housing, retail frontage, plazas, or bike/transit lanes in a dense core than as car storage and throughput. Cities that have done this — Ghent, Pontevedra, large chunks of central Paris, Oslo's inner zone — haven't seen the economic collapse that opponents predict. Foot traffic and local retail revenue typically go up, not down, once you're not fighting for space with traffic.

**Why I don't think this is a close call:** The usual counterarguments (elderly/disabled mobility, deliveries, emergency access, businesses that depend on car traffic) are all solvable with exceptions and permits, not reasons to abandon the policy. They're implementation details, not fatal flaws. The much harder problem is transit quality — a ban is unfair if the alternative is a bad bus system — but that's an argument for sequencing (build transit first or simultaneously), not an argument against the goal.

**Where I'd push back on the "obviously yes" crowd:** This only works well in cities with real population density and decent existing transit bones. Transplanting Amsterdam's playbook onto a sprawling car-dependent American Sunbelt city without first building rail or dense housing near the core is likely to just strangle the downtown rather than transform it. So my actual view has a condition attached: yes, ban them — but treat transit investment as the prerequisite, not an optional add-on, and phase it in over years so businesses and residents can adjust.

If pushed to state it in one line: it's good policy that's been under-tried because of political cowardice, not because the evidence is ambiguous.
