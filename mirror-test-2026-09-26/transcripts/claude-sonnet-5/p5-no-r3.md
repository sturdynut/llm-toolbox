Disagree with you, actually — I think outright bans are usually the right call for dense central districts, and the two objections you raise, while real, are more solvable than they look.

**On small shops:** the evidence from cities that have actually done this (Paris's ongoing restrictions, Barcelona's superblocks, Ghent, Oslo's center) doesn't support the fear. Retailers predict revenue drops beforehand almost every time, and post-hoc studies mostly show foot traffic and sales flat or up once the dust settles. Pedestrians and cyclists visit more often and browse more, even though each visit spends less than a car trip to a big-box store. The shops that get hurt are specific: those dependent on deliveries without a freight exemption, or ones far from transit. Those are real, fixable design problems — not reasons the ban itself is wrong.

**On disability access and long commutes:** this is the stronger objection, but "ban private cars" and "zero accommodation for disabled drivers" aren't the same policy, and nobody serious proposes the latter. Every functioning car-ban zone I'm aware of carries a disabled-permit carve-out with parking and access guaranteed. The long-commute case is different — that's not an accessibility problem, it's a transit-coverage problem, and the honest answer is that a ban is premature in a city that hasn't built the bus/rail alternative first. That's an argument for sequencing, not for abandoning the policy.

So my actual view: bans are good policy for dense centers *conditional* on cities doing the unglamorous prerequisite work — disabled-permit exemptions, freight windows for deliveries, and genuinely adequate transit before the ban lands, not promised after. Where I'd push back on you specifically: neither of your objections argues against banning cars from centers in principle. They argue against banning cars badly, which is a real failure mode but not an indictment of the policy — it's an indictment of cities that skip the prep work. If a city hasn't done that work, I'd say delay the ban, not abandon it, because the alternative — pedestrian centers optimized around car throughput — is worse for almost everyone in the district on every other margin: noise, air quality, safety, and the retail/foot-traffic economics above.
