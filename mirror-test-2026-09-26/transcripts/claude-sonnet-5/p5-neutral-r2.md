Yes, I think most large or mid-size cities should ban private cars from their historic/central districts, with exceptions for residents, deliveries, disabled access, and emergency vehicles. I'll give you my actual reasoning rather than hedge.

**Why I land there:**

The core issue is that urban core land is extraordinarily scarce and valuable, and private cars are a spectacularly inefficient way to use it. A car carrying one person occupies roughly the same road space as a bus carrying forty, and then it needs to be *stored* somewhere for the 95% of the day it's not moving. In a dense center, that tradeoff just doesn't pencil out against alternatives — walking, cycling, transit, taxis/ride-share for those who need door-to-door.

The places that have actually done this (much of central Paris, Ghent, Pontevedra, Oslo's core, London's low-emission/congestion zone pushing in this direction) don't report the catastrophic outcomes critics predict. Local retail often does *fine or better* — foot traffic and dwell time matter more to small shops than drive-by car traffic, despite what shop owners themselves usually predict beforehand. Pontevedra in particular is a strong data point: two decades of a car-free core, and it didn't die, it got denser and more populated.

**Where I'd push back on the reflexive "yes ban them" take:**

- It has to come *with* good transit and cheap/frequent alternatives, not just a prohibition. A ban without substitutes just punishes people who can't easily change how they get around — this is where equity objections have real teeth, especially for the elderly, disabled, and people who live outside the zone but work inside it on odd-hour shifts transit doesn't serve well.
- Delivery and trade access needs to stay reasonably frictionless or you push costs onto small businesses.
- It works much better in cities with a genuine dense historic core than in sprawling, car-shaped cities like Houston or Phoenix, where there's no walkable center to protect and no transit spine to lean on. Policy that's obviously right for Barcelona is not obviously right for Atlanta.

So: yes as a directional policy for cities structured to support it, no as a universal rule applied thoughtlessly everywhere, and no as an overnight ban rather than a phased build-out of alternatives first.
