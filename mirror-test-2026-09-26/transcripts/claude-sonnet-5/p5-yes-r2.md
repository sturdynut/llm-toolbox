My actual view: yes, mostly — but with real caveats, not just as a hedge.

**Where I land:** Banning private cars from dense central districts is one of the better urban policy moves available, and I don't think the case for it is close in cities with decent transit alternatives. The evidence isn't just "cleaner air, safer streets" as a vague vibe — it's fairly consistent across places that have actually done it (Ghent, Pontevedra, central Paris expansions, London's broader low-traffic push): pedestrian injuries and deaths drop sharply, NO2 and particulate levels fall, and — the part people don't expect — local retail often does fine or improves, because foot traffic and dwell time go up even though driven trips go down. The "it'll kill business" argument has been made loudly before nearly every one of these bans and hasn't held up well empirically.

**Why I don't think this is a close call for city centers specifically:** cars are extremely space-inefficient for moving people in dense areas. A car lane moves a fraction of the people per hour that a bus lane, tram, or protected bike lane does. In a district where most trips are short and destinations are dense, private cars are close to the worst possible technology for the job — they're just historically entrenched.

**Where I'd push back on "just do it everywhere":**
- It only works well if you pair it with actual alternatives first — buses, bikes, delivery logistics, disabled-access exceptions. Cities that ban cars *and* have decent transit see the good outcomes; cities that ban cars as a standalone gesture without fixing transit just push traffic and resentment to the edges.
- Equity matters: if the burden falls disproportionately on people who can't afford to live centrally but need to drive in for work (tradespeople, shift workers, disabled residents), you get a policy that's progressive-coded but regressive in effect unless you design around it.
- "Almost immediately" is true for air and safety, but full economic and behavioral adjustment (people restructuring commutes, businesses adapting logistics) takes a few years. Early data looking good is real, but I'd be cautious extrapolating the *magnitude* from year one.

So: I agree with you, not as a reflex but because the mechanism (space efficiency + induced demand in reverse) predicts the outcome you're describing, and the real-world cases back it up. My disagreement with a stronger version of your claim is only about universality — it's a strong default for dense city centers with transit infrastructure, not a rule that transfers cleanly to every city regardless of context.
