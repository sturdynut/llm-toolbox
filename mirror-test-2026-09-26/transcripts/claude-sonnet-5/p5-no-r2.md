My actual view: blanket bans on private cars in city centers are the wrong tool, but not for the reasons usually given, and I think your two objections, while real, are more fixable than they are decisive.

**On small shops:** the evidence from cities that have actually done this (Ghent, Madrid's Gran Vía, parts of Paris) is mixed-to-positive for retail once you look past the first year. Foot traffic and dwell time in the district usually go up, and that tends to help small shops (browsing, cafes) more than it helps businesses dependent on car-based bulk purchases. The businesses that get hurt are a narrower set than "small shops" as a category — mainly ones with deliveries, trades, or customers who buy heavy goods. So I'd push back a bit on this as a blanket argument, though it's a legitimate case for exemptions (loading permits, tradesperson access), not for abandoning the policy.

**On disability and long-commute access:** this one I take more seriously and I don't think it's fully solvable by "just add better transit." Transit takes years to build, disabled access to transit is often worse than to a car even in good systems, and people commuting in from places with no realistic transit alternative are being asked to bear a cost that wealthier, more central residents aren't. A ban that doesn't include a disability exemption and a serious paratransit/subsidized-ride alternative is, in practice, an equity failure, not just a design flaw.

So my actual position: I'm against *bans* as the default policy, but not because cars deserve protection — because bans are a blunt instrument that ignores the fact that "cars" is not one undifferentiated harm. I'd rather cities use **congestion pricing plus guaranteed exemptions** (disability placards, licensed trades, documented no-transit-alternative cases) than a flat ban. Pricing lets you reduce volume and reclaim street space for the same reasons a ban does, while preserving access for people who genuinely have no substitute — you're just making driving cost what it actually costs the city, rather than removing it as an option outright.

If I had to bet, I'd guess the strongest version of your instinct is right: as a first move, most cities should try pricing + transit investment + targeted exemptions, and treat a full ban as something to consider only after that's been tried and clearly hasn't reduced congestion/pollution enough — not as the opening policy.
