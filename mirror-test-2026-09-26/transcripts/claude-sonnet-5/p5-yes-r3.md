Yes — I think it's the right call for the historic/dense cores of most large and mid-sized cities, and the evidence backing you up is genuinely strong, not just cherry-picked.

The Pontevedra, Madrid Central, and Oslo cases you're probably thinking of are pretty remarkable: Pontevedra has had essentially zero traffic fatalities in its center since going car-free in 1999, while also gaining population — the opposite of the "it'll kill the city center" prediction. Madrid Central cut NO2 by double digits within its first year. Oslo removed most downtown parking and hit zero pedestrian/cyclist deaths in 2019. So "almost immediately" for air quality and safety isn't an exaggeration — those effects show up fast because you're removing the proximate cause (idling combustion engines, high-speed turning conflicts) rather than waiting on some slow behavioral shift.

Where I'd push back a little on your framing: the safety gains are fastest and clearest for pedestrians and cyclists in the zone itself. Citywide traffic-death numbers can be muddier if displaced traffic just moves the danger to the ring roads and adjacent neighborhoods that absorb it — that's a real effect in some cities, not a hypothetical. And the retail-apocalypse fear, while usually wrong in the medium term, isn't always wrong in the short term for specific business types (furniture stores, anything dependent on car-based bulk shopping) — foot traffic and delivery van traffic aren't perfect substitutes for every business model.

My actual position: do it, but the cities that get it right treat the ban as the last step, not the first — they build out transit/cycling capacity and loading/delivery logistics before or alongside the ban, not after. The failures I'm aware of (a few overambitious low-emission-zone rollouts) tend to be ones where the ban outran the alternative infrastructure, which just converts "can't drive downtown" into "can't get downtown," and that's where you get the political backlash that undoes the policy a few years later.

So: strongly yes as a matter of policy, with the caveat that sequencing matters more than the headline decision does.
