My view is that **well-designed car restrictions in dense city centers are usually a good idea, but blanket bans with no exceptions are not.** So I partly disagree with you. Your two concerns deserve different answers.

**On small shops, I think the evidence mostly cuts against you.** Merchants consistently overestimate how many customers arrive by car. Studies in Graz, Bristol, Toronto, and elsewhere found shopkeepers guessing roughly double the real share. Pedestrians and cyclists spend less per trip but visit more often, and often spend as much or more per month. Places that restricted cars, such as Pontevedra, central Oslo, Ghent, and Madrid, generally saw foot traffic and retail hold steady or improve after an adjustment period. There are real losers: businesses selling bulky goods, and shops during the transition. But "bans kill small shops" isn't the typical outcome.

**On disability and long commutes, you're pointing at something real,** and it's the strongest objection. A crude ban that treats a wheelchair user the same as a solo commuter in an SUV is unjust. But I think this is an argument for good design rather than against the policy. The successful schemes include:
- permits or exemptions for disabled drivers and taxis/paratransit
- access for deliveries, trades, and residents
- park-and-ride at the edges and frequent transit into the zone
- a small restricted core rather than a whole city

Without those, I'd oppose a scheme too.

**Why I come down in favor overall:** central districts are where space is scarcest and where cars impose the most on others, through congestion, noise, air pollution, pedestrian injuries, and land devoted to parking. Most people entering dense centers already don't drive. Restrictions let a street serve far more people than it can when it's full of cars.

**Where I'd change my mind:** a city with weak transit, a sprawling layout, or a "center" that people must drive *through* to reach other places. There, a ban mostly shifts burdens onto people without alternatives.

So my honest position is: yes to restrictions in places that meet those conditions, provided the exemptions and transit are in place first. I think your concerns are best treated as design requirements rather than reasons to reject the idea.
