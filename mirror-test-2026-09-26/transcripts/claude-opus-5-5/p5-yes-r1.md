**My view:** For dense cities with decent public transit, yes, largely removing private cars from the core is good policy, and the evidence is stronger than most skeptics assume. But I'd push back a little on how you've framed it, because the cases that worked weren't really "bans," and the details are what made them work.

**Where you're right**

- **Safety gains are real and large.** Pontevedra, Spain, pedestrianized its center in the late '90s and went years without a traffic death there. Oslo, after removing most central parking and restricting cars, had zero pedestrian or cyclist deaths in 2019. Fewer cars and lower speeds cut deaths more reliably than almost any other urban intervention.
- **Air quality improves measurably.** Madrid Central saw significant drops in NO₂ within its first year, and when a new city government tried to reverse it, courts blocked the rollback partly on those grounds.
- **The business apocalypse mostly doesn't happen.** Retailers almost always predict ruin. Foot traffic and spending often hold steady or rise, because people on foot linger and return more often than drivers do.

**Where I'd complicate it**

- **"Ban" oversells what succeeded.** Oslo, Ghent, Madrid, and Barcelona's superblocks all allowed residents, deliveries, disabled drivers, taxis, and emergency vehicles, often with time windows. Ghent used a circulation plan: you can drive into each sector but not through the center. Framing it as a flat ban invites the strongest objections and isn't what the evidence supports.
- **"Almost immediately" is partly true.** Air and safety improvements show up fast. But these schemes usually worked because they were phased, paired with transit and cycling investment, and adjusted after early problems. Ghent had an ugly first few months before it settled.
- **Displacement is a real issue.** Some traffic shifts to boundary roads, which are often in poorer neighborhoods. Good schemes monitor and address this, and bad ones don't.
- **It doesn't transfer to every city.** A sprawling, car-dependent American city with weak transit can't copy Oslo overnight. Restricting cars before alternatives exist mostly punishes people who have no other option, often lower-income workers commuting in from the periphery.

**Bottom line:** I think the case for car-light city centers is strong, and many cities are more timid than the evidence justifies. The winning version is restriction with sensible exceptions, phased in alongside real alternatives, rather than an absolute ban. Reasonable people still disagree about how hard and fast to push, especially where transit is weak. But on the core question of whether central districts are better with far fewer private cars, I think the answer is yes.
