**My view: yes, for most dense city centers, cars should be heavily restricted, though "ban" is usually the wrong word and the wrong design.** The evidence broadly supports you, but the successful cases look different from a blanket ban.

**Where I agree with you**

The results are real and often fast. Pontevedra, Spain, pedestrianized its core in the late 1990s, and traffic deaths fell to near zero while the center repopulated. Oslo removed most central parking and through-traffic, and in 2019 recorded zero pedestrian or cyclist deaths citywide. Madrid Central produced measurable drops in NO₂ within months, and when a new city government tried to reverse it, courts and public pressure largely kept it. Ghent's 2017 circulation plan, which blocked cars from crossing the center rather than banning them, cut central traffic sharply and increased cycling.

The feared downsides also tend not to materialize. Retailers almost always predict ruin and usually do fine or better, because pedestrians linger and spend more often than drivers do. Traffic also partly "evaporates" rather than just shifting to surrounding roads, because some trips change mode, time, or destination, or simply stop happening.

**Where I'd push back a little**

- **Most successes weren't outright bans.** They kept access for residents, deliveries (often in time windows), disabled drivers, emergency vehicles, and trades. Oslo mostly removed parking rather than cars. Ghent used a loop system. These exemptions are a large part of why the policies worked and survived politically.
- **Selection effect.** The cities that did it had compact, walkable cores and decent transit already. Doing the same in a sprawling city with weak transit would hurt people who have no alternative, often lower-income workers commuting in from outer areas, before it helped anyone.
- **Sequencing matters.** Where restrictions came before good transit and cycling alternatives, the backlash was worse and some policies were rolled back.

**So my actual position:** in dense cores with reasonable transit, which describes most European cities and a fair number elsewhere, cities should restrict private through-traffic and most parking, with sensible exemptions, and should do it more boldly than they tend to. The safety and air-quality gains are large, and the costs are mostly transitional and overestimated in advance. In car-dependent cities, I'd start with transit, parking reform, and pedestrianizing specific streets, and treat full restriction as a later step.

Reasonable people weigh these tradeoffs differently, especially around personal freedom and who bears the transition costs. But on the core empirical question of whether it makes city centers safer, cleaner, and more livable, I think the evidence is on your side.
