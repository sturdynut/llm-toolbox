My view is that dense city centers with decent transit generally *should* heavily restrict private cars, but through well-designed restrictions with real exemptions rather than blunt blanket bans. Your two concerns are legitimate, but I think one is mostly contradicted by evidence and the other is a design problem rather than a reason to reject the idea.

**On small shops, the evidence mostly cuts the other way.** Merchants consistently overestimate how many of their customers arrive by car. Surveys in cities like Graz, Bristol, Toronto, and Berlin found shopkeepers guessing roughly double the real share. People on foot and bike tend to spend less per visit but come more often, and often spend as much or more per month. Outcomes from pedestrianized or car-restricted areas (Madrid Central, Pontevedra, Oslo's center, many European high streets) have generally shown steady or rising retail activity, not collapse. There are real exceptions: businesses selling bulky goods, or districts where transit is poor and nothing replaces the lost access. So "bans hurt shops" is true only under specific conditions, not as a rule.

**On disability and long commutes, you're pointing at the real risk.** A policy that simply locks out a wheelchair user, or a shift worker from an underserved suburb, is a bad policy. But good schemes handle this directly:
- Permits for disabled drivers and passengers
- Door-to-door paratransit
- Access for deliveries, trades, taxis, and residents
- Park-and-ride at the edge, plus transit that runs when shift workers actually need it

When cities skip these, the criticism is deserved. But that argues for doing restrictions properly, not against doing them.

**Why I land on the side of restriction:** Central districts are where space is scarcest and where cars impose the most on everyone else, through congestion, noise, air pollution, and pedestrian injuries. Most people in those areas already arrive another way, so the curb space goes to a minority. Freeing it up tends to make centers more pleasant, safer, and often more commercially vibrant.

**Where I'd agree with you:** A total ban in a mid-sized, car-dependent city with weak transit would likely hurt people and businesses. Sequencing matters. Build the alternatives first, then restrict. Congestion pricing or restricted zones with exemptions are usually wiser than an outright ban.

So I'd reframe your position slightly. The strongest version of your argument isn't "don't restrict cars" but "don't restrict cars carelessly." On that, I think you're right.
