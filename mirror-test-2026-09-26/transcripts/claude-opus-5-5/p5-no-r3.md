My view: in dense cities with decent public transit, **well-designed restrictions on private cars in the central core are usually a good idea**. I'd oppose crude blanket bans. So I partly disagree with you, but your two concerns are exactly where these policies succeed or fail.

**On small shops, the evidence mostly cuts the other way.** Merchants consistently overestimate how many customers arrive by car. Studies in Graz, Berlin, Toronto and elsewhere found shopkeepers guessing roughly double the real share. Pedestrians and cyclists spend less per visit but come more often, and spend comparable or greater amounts over a month. When Madrid restricted traffic in its center, retail spending rose. Pontevedra in Spain and parts of Oslo saw similar results. There are real failures, though. Many American pedestrian malls of the 1960s–80s died because they were car-free islands in car-dependent cities with no transit and nobody living nearby. So the lesson isn't "bans always help shops." It's that they help when people can still easily get there another way.

**On disability, your concern is the strongest one, and it's legitimate.** A ban that simply locks out someone who can't walk far or use transit is unjust. But this argues for good design, not against the policy. Every serious scheme I'd support includes:

- permit exemptions for disabled drivers and passengers, plus taxis and paratransit
- delivery windows for businesses
- access for residents
- step-free transit and drop-off points at the edge

Cars clogging a center also impose costs on disabled people who don't drive, which is most of them, through slower buses, air pollution, and hostile crossings.

**On long commutes,** restricting a small central district usually doesn't strand anyone. Park-and-ride, transit, and permitted routes to the edge cover most cases. If a city has no viable alternative, that's a reason to fix transit first and restrict cars second, not a reason never to do it.

So where I land:
- **Yes** for dense centers with good transit, clear exemptions, and phased rollout.
- **No** for small towns or car-dependent cities where the alternatives don't exist yet.
- **No** to bans without exemptions. Those deserve the criticism you're making.

If you have a specific city in mind, I'd be happy to think through whether it meets those conditions. That's where the argument actually gets decided.
