**My view: for most large, dense cities with decent transit, yes, restricting private cars in the core is a good idea. But an outright ban is often not the best tool, and the details matter more than the headline.**

**Why I lean yes**

- **Space is the core argument.** Central districts are the most valuable land a city has, and private cars use it badly. A parked car occupies about 12 m² for roughly 95% of the day. A moving car carries typically 1.2 people in space that could move many more by bus, bike, or foot. In a dense core, that's hard to justify.
- **The evidence from places that have done it is fairly consistent.** Pontevedra, Oslo's center, Ghent's circulation plan, Madrid Central, Barcelona's superblocks, and Paris's riverfront changes all showed large drops in pollution and traffic injuries. The feared commercial collapse mostly didn't happen, and foot traffic and retail often improved. Merchants reliably overestimate how many of their customers arrive by car.
- **Traffic tends to evaporate rather than simply relocate.** It doesn't vanish entirely, but the "displacement will gridlock everything else" prediction usually turns out overstated.

**Where I'd push back on a blanket ban**

- **Congestion pricing is often better.** London, Stockholm, Singapore, and now New York show that charging for access gets much of the benefit while preserving flexibility for trips that really need a car. It also raises money for transit. A ban is blunter. It's cleaner to enforce and more transformative for street design, but less efficient.
- **Exemptions are essential.** Disabled access, deliveries (ideally time-windowed), emergency services, trades, and residents in some form. Schemes that ignore these generate justified backlash and rightly so.
- **Sequencing matters.** Restricting cars before transit alternatives are good just punishes people, disproportionately those commuting in from poorer peripheries. Improve the alternatives first or simultaneously.
- **Context matters.** This makes much more sense for Paris or Copenhagen than for a mid-sized American city with a hollow downtown and weak transit. There, a ban could accelerate decline rather than reverse it.

**Bottom line:** I think the default in dense city centers should shift decisively away from private cars. Cities that have done so rarely reverse course, which is telling. But I'd usually favor pricing plus targeted car-free zones over a total ban, paired with real investment in alternatives.

Reasonable people weigh the tradeoffs differently, especially on how much to prioritize individual flexibility against shared space. Where I've landed is that in dense cores, the shared-space argument wins.
