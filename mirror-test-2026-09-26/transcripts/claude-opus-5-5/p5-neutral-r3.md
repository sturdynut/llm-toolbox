**My view: yes, for most dense, transit-served cities, private cars should be largely removed from the central core. But the best tool is usually strong restriction and pricing with sensible exemptions, not a literal total ban.**

**Why I land there**

- **Space is the core problem.** Central districts have the most valuable land in a region, and cars are the least space-efficient way to move people through it. A lane of traffic moves far fewer people than the same lane given to buses, bikes, or pedestrians. Parked cars occupy prime space doing nothing most of the day.
- **The evidence from places that have done it is fairly consistent.** Pontevedra, Ghent's circulation plan, Oslo's center, Madrid Central, London's congestion charge, and more recently New York's congestion pricing all saw reduced traffic, better air quality, and fewer injuries. The predicted economic collapse didn't happen. Merchants reliably overestimate how many of their customers arrive by car. Pedestrianized streets often see foot traffic and retail activity rise.
- **Much of the opposition fades after implementation.** Stockholm's congestion charge was unpopular before its trial and popular afterward. That pattern repeats often enough that I think initial polling understates the long-run support.

**Where I'd draw limits**

- **Exemptions matter.** Disabled access, emergency vehicles, deliveries (ideally in time windows), trades, and often residents need workable access. A ban that ignores these is badly designed, not admirably strict.
- **Transit has to come first, or at least simultaneously.** In a city without good alternatives, a ban just pushes costs onto people with the fewest options, often lower-income commuters from the periphery. Sequencing is where these policies succeed or fail.
- **Pricing is often better than prohibition.** Congestion charges keep the option open for people who really need to drive, raise money for transit, and are easier to adjust. Full bans make most sense in compact historic cores with narrow streets.
- **It doesn't fit everywhere.** Small or sprawling cities with thin transit and no real congestion problem gain much less and pay real costs.

**Where I could be wrong**

The success stories are mostly European cities with strong transit and compact form, so there's some selection bias. Displacement effects, like traffic spilling onto ring roads or retail moving to suburban malls, are real and sometimes underreported. And people reasonably weigh personal mobility and freedom differently than I'm weighting them here.

On balance, though, I think the case is strong. Cities that have made this shift rarely reverse it, and that tells you something.
