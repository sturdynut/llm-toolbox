**My view: yes, for most dense cities with decent transit, central districts should be largely car-free.** But the version I'd support is more careful than a blanket ban, and the evidence is a bit messier than "cleaner and safer almost immediately."

**Why I lean yes**

- **The safety case is the strongest.** Pontevedra in Spain pedestrianized its core in the late 1990s and went years with essentially no traffic deaths there. Oslo had near-zero pedestrian and cyclist deaths in 2019 after removing central parking and restricting cars. When you remove fast, heavy vehicles from places full of people, fewer people die. That's hard to argue with.
- **Air quality improves measurably.** Madrid Central cut NO₂ at central monitoring stations noticeably. London's congestion charge and ULEZ show similar patterns.
- **Central districts are where cars make the least sense.** Space is scarcest, walking distances are short, and transit is densest. A car stored or moving there uses a large share of public space to serve one or two people.
- **The feared economic collapse rarely happens.** Shopkeepers consistently overestimate how many customers arrive by car. Foot traffic often rises after pedestrianization, though there's usually a rough adjustment period.

**Where I'd push back on your framing**

- **Most successes aren't pure bans.** Oslo, Ghent, Madrid, and Barcelona's superblocks mostly use removed parking, circulation plans that block through-traffic, low-emission rules, or charges, with exemptions for residents, deliveries, disabled drivers, and emergency vehicles. Those exemptions are a big part of why the policies work and survive politically.
- **"Almost immediately" oversells it.** Some gains, like fewer crashes in the zone, appear fast. Others depend on how traffic shifts afterward. Some displaces to the zone's edges, and the net citywide effect is usually positive but smaller than the headline in-zone numbers.
- **Some of the gains come from other policies.** Helsinki's zero-pedestrian-death year owed a lot to lower speed limits, not bans. Cities that restrict cars often also invest in transit and cycling, so it's hard to credit the restriction alone.

**Where I'd hold back**

In sprawling, car-dependent cities with weak transit, a hard ban mostly punishes people who have no alternative, often lower-income commuters and people with disabilities. There, I'd start with transit, parking reform, and lower speeds, and restrict cars as alternatives become real.

So: I agree with your conclusion for dense cities, and I think the best approach is phased restriction with sensible exemptions rather than an absolute ban. Reasonable people weigh access, cost, and personal freedom differently, but on safety and livability I think the evidence favors doing it.
