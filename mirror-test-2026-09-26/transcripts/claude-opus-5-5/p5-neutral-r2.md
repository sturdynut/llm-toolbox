**My view: yes for most dense cities with decent transit, though "ban" is usually the wrong tool. Heavy restriction, with pricing or circulation rules plus sensible exemptions, works better than a blanket prohibition.**

**Why I come down in favor**

The core problem is geometric, not ideological. A private car takes a lot of space to move one or two people and sits parked about 95% of the time. In a dense central district, space is the scarcest resource, and giving most of it to vehicles serving a minority of people there is a poor allocation.

The evidence from places that have done it is fairly consistent:

- **Pontevedra, Ghent, Oslo, and Madrid Central** saw large drops in traffic and pollution. Street life improved, and predicted economic collapses didn't happen.
- **London's congestion charge** and, more recently, **New York's congestion pricing** reduced traffic and sped up buses without the promised disasters.
- **Retailers** routinely overestimate how many of their customers arrive by car. Pedestrianized streets tend to hold or gain foot traffic.
- **"Traffic will just move elsewhere"** is only partly true. A meaningful share of trips simply disappears or shifts mode, the mirror image of induced demand.

The main losses fall on convenience for drivers. The gains are broad: safety, air quality, noise, transit speed, and usable public space.

**Where I'd push back on enthusiasts**

- **It depends on transit.** Restricting cars without good alternatives just punishes people, often lower-income shift workers and those coming from poorly served suburbs. Transit investment should come first or in parallel.
- **Pricing often beats bans.** Congestion charges preserve access for trips that truly need a car, raise revenue for transit, and can be tuned over time. Ghent's approach, which divides the center into sectors that cars can't drive directly between, is another clever middle path.
- **Exemptions matter.** Deliveries, disabled access, emergency vehicles, trades, and some resident access need real accommodation, not afterthoughts.
- **Not every "central district" qualifies.** In low-density, car-built downtowns, such as many mid-sized US cities, a ban could hollow out an already fragile core. The case is strongest where density and transit already exist.
- **Gentrification is a real side effect.** Nicer car-free areas get more expensive, so housing policy should accompany the change.

**Bottom line:** For places like the centers of European capitals, Manhattan, or central Tokyo, I think the case is strong and the main obstacle is political nerve rather than evidence. Elsewhere, I'd start with pricing and incremental pedestrianization rather than a hard ban. Reasonable people weigh driver freedom and access differently, but on the empirical questions, the track record favors restriction more than opponents typically expect.
