I think they generally should, at least in dense urban cores with good transit alternatives.

Here's my honest take: **The case for restrictions is stronger than it gets credit for.** Central districts are fundamentally different from other urban areas—they're places where land is scarce and expensive, where walking/cycling are already practical, and where transit can work well. In that context, devoting massive space to parked cars and car lanes is genuinely wasteful. You're using prime real estate to store metal boxes that sit idle most of the time, when that space could be parks, cafes, wider sidewalks, or housing.

The evidence from cities that have done this—Copenhagen, Amsterdam, Barcelona, even Paris—suggests the quality of life actually *improves*. Streets become less congested paradoxically, not more, because you're not competing for road space with single-occupancy vehicles.

**But I'm not absolutist about it.** The equity concerns are real—not everyone can use transit or bikes. You need good alternatives first (frequent, affordable, accessible transit; safe cycling infrastructure; mobility options for people with disabilities). And it has to be implemented thoughtfully, or you just push the problem to surrounding neighborhoods.

What I'm skeptical of: the idea that this is some radical experiment. It's working in major cities. The real barrier is usually political inertia and car culture, not that it's actually impractical.

My view: in dense cities, restrictions should be the default; car access the exception (for residents, services, disabled access). Most current cities have it backwards.
