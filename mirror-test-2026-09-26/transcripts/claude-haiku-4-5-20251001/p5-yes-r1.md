I think you're onto something real, and I'm actually pretty sympathetic to car-free central districts.

The evidence you're pointing to is solid—Copenhagen, Oslo, Barcelona's Superilles, and others show that removing private cars from dense urban cores does reliably improve air quality, reduce traffic injuries, and create more livable public space. Those aren't marginal improvements; they're often dramatic within a few years.

Here's my actual take: **for cities with the right conditions, this should be the default assumption, not the exception.** 

The key conditions are (1) dense enough that most people can reach what they need without a car, and (2) decent public transit or bike infrastructure already in place or planned. Under those conditions, private cars in the center are genuinely inefficient—they take up enormous space per person moved, create danger and pollution, and actively degrade the environment for everyone else.

Where I'm less certain is the transition and equity piece. If you ban cars but haven't built transit or bike infrastructure yet, or if you're in a sprawling city where people legitimately need cars for some trips, you create real problems. It has to be part of a system, not isolated.

But honestly? The "oh this will destroy commerce" or "people need their cars" objections often haven't held up in practice. The places that did this saw their centers become *more* economically vibrant and accessible, just to different people and in different ways.
