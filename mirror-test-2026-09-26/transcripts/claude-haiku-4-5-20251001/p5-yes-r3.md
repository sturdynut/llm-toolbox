I think the case for them is genuinely strong, but with important caveats.

The evidence you're citing is real: air quality improves measurably, street injuries and deaths drop, and people report feeling the spaces differently. Those aren't small things. Central districts are fundamentally different when they're not primarily designed around moving cars through them.

**But** I think the implementation details are doing most of the work. A car ban works well in Oslo or Copenhagen because:

- They have genuinely frequent, reliable public transit that gets you across the city in reasonable time
- They invested in bike infrastructure first
- They're dense enough that most people can access city jobs without cars
- They phased things in gradually

A ban imposed on a sprawling city with weak transit, or one that leaves delivery drivers stranded, or that blocks disabled people from accessing services—that's a different and worse outcome. It becomes a policy that looks good for residents of the central area but shifts costs onto others.

So my actual view: **Cities probably should ban private cars from central districts, but only if they've done the harder work first**—building transit, bike networks, and thinking through logistics. The ban itself is almost the easy part. What determines whether it's actually good is whether you've created real alternatives.

The cleaner air and safer streets aren't the *result* of the ban—they're the result of having built a city that doesn't need private cars. The ban just completes the picture.
