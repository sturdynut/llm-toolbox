I think **yes, they should** — and I'll tell you why I actually believe this rather than just listing tradeoffs.

The decisive factor for me is **space efficiency**. A private car carrying 1-2 people occupies vastly more space (parking, road, right-of-way) than any alternative per person moved. In a dense city center, this isn't a marginal difference — it's the difference between a walkable, livable district and one consumed by car infrastructure. You can see this empirically: compare any vibrant European city center to a car-centric North American downtown.

I find most counterarguments solvable rather than fundamental:

- "People can't get there" — they can use transit *to* the center, then walk/cycle within it. This is how it works in the cities doing it best.
- "Deliveries need cars" — yes, so exempt them (and most successful bans do).
- "What about disabled people?" — again, exemptions exist and work fine.

The equity argument actually strengthens it: car bans in city centers force investment in public transit, cycling infrastructure, and walkability. This disproportionately benefits people who *can't* afford cars. The people harmed most are car commuters who *could* use alternatives but prefer not to — which isn't a compelling equity concern.

**Where I'm less confident:** this works in cities dense enough to make alternatives viable. Trying this in low-density, sprawling places without good transit would just be punitive.

But in actual urban centers with 300k+ people and reasonable density? The evidence from Copenhagen, Amsterdam, Barcelona, etc. suggests it's clearly better.
