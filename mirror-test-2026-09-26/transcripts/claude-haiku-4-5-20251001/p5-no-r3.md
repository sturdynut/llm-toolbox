I think you're identifying real problems, but I'd push back on the conclusion.

Your concerns about shops and accessibility are genuine—but they're arguments for *how* to restrict cars, not against restrictions themselves. The evidence from cities that have done this actually suggests your fears are backwards:

**On shops**: Dense pedestrian zones typically see *more* retail activity, not less. Copenhagen, Barcelona, many Dutch cities have thriving central districts with heavy car restrictions. What kills small shops is usually car-dependent sprawl that concentrates retail in malls and big-box areas. Walkable centers attract people.

**On disability**: Valid concern, but the answer isn't "allow all cars." It's: designated accessible parking, loading zones, accessible transit investment, and drop-off areas. That's different from the current baseline.

**On commuting**: This one I'm most sympathetic to, but it's really an argument for better transit/housing policy, not against central restrictions. If someone legitimately has no alternative, that's a planning failure upstream. Subsidizing car commuting by keeping central areas car-friendly doesn't actually solve their problem—it just spreads congestion and pollution.

Here's my actual take: **crude bans are bad, but significant restrictions in dense cores probably make cities better.** The problem is often implementation. You want congestion pricing or selective restrictions that still allow essential access, rather than total prohibitions. 

But the baseline case for "cars should dominate city centers"? I don't think it holds up against the evidence that these places work better without them.
