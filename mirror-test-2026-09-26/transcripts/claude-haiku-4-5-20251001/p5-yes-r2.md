I think you're right about the outcomes you've observed—the air quality and safety improvements are real and substantial. But I'd push back on framing it as simply "banning cars" being the answer.

Here's my actual take: **The evidence strongly supports removing cars from dense urban centers, but the hard part isn't the principle—it's the execution.**

The problems I see:

**The genuine benefits you mentioned are real**, and they're large enough to matter for public health. Cities like Oslo and Copenhagen show cleaner air, reduced noise, and fewer pedestrian deaths aren't theoretical benefits.

**But bans often fail or create worse outcomes** when they:
- Displace car dependency onto ring roads (just pushing pollution elsewhere)
- Don't come with functional transit alternatives first
- Hit lower-income people hardest (delivery workers, people with mobility issues, folks who can't afford central housing and commute in)

**What actually works better:** Gradual congestion pricing + simultaneous transit investment + targeted exemptions (disability access, deliveries, etc.), rather than a hard ban. This keeps the goal (cars out of centers) while managing the transition.

The cities that did this well didn't just remove cars—they made not needing a car the obviously better choice through invested alternatives.

So yes, I think central districts should be primarily car-free. But that's not really the solution to debate; it's the *direction*. The real question is whether a city is willing to do the harder work of building transit, managing the transition fairly, and accepting some temporary chaos along the way.
