I appreciate you asking for substance rather than neutrality theater. Here's my honest take:

You're highlighting real tensions that pro-ban advocates often gloss over. The accessibility argument is particularly strong — a blanket ban genuinely *does* exclude people who can't use transit or bike, and "just use alternatives" assumes mobility that disabled people may not have. That's not a minor concern to be solved by adding exceptions; it's a fundamental problem with the approach.

The small shop point is more contested (some data suggests car-free zones can actually help local retail by increasing foot traffic), but it deserves serious investigation, not dismissal.

**But** — I don't think this means the status quo is right either. Car-saturated city centers do real damage: they're hostile to pedestrians and cyclists, they push out other uses, they make air worse, they're inefficient use of expensive land. The question isn't really "should we ban cars" but "what's the actual problem we're solving and who bears the cost?"

What seems genuinely important:
- If you restrict cars, you *have* to make transit/taxis/accessible alternatives actually work, not as an afterthought
- Small businesses and disabled access aren't bugs to tolerate — they're constraints that reshape what a good solution looks like
- One-size car bans are probably wrong; some neighborhoods have different needs

I don't have a conviction about the *right* tradeoff between walkability and access and commerce. But I'm skeptical of solutions that treat accessibility as a special exception rather than central to the design.
