I think your concerns are real, but they're actually arguments for *how* to do car restrictions well—not against doing them at all.

Here's my honest take:

**You're right that badly-designed bans are harmful.** If a city just prohibits cars and leaves it at that, yeah, small retailers suffer and people without transit alternatives get screwed. That's bad policy.

**But the evidence from cities that did this thoughtfully is pretty encouraging.** Oslo, parts of Copenhagen, many European city centers—they paired restrictions with: serious transit investment, bike infrastructure, accessible shuttle services, and disability accommodations. Retail often bounced back in these places; they became more vibrant, not less.

**On your specific concerns:**
- The disability one gets oversimplified. Many disabled people *prefer* car-free areas (no exhaust, quieter, fewer barriers). The real need is good paratransit and accessibility design, which works *better* without car conflicts.
- Long commutes are real, but that's a sprawl/housing problem, not really solved by letting cars clog city centers anyway.
- Small shops do need foot traffic, but dense, walkable car-free zones *generate* foot traffic.

**What I actually think:** Cities should reduce car dependency in centers, but only as part of a package—not as an isolated ban that punishes people. The problem isn't the goal; it's half-measures.

Your concern about excluding people is the right concern. It just points toward: do it comprehensively, not do nothing.
